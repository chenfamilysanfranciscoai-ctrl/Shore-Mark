Understanding the habitats

Volunteering goes further when you know why the work matters. These are short, general summaries, and the links go to groups that know the details.

Beaches and shoreline

Trash and plastic reach the coast from storm drains, creeks and the water itself. Cleanups remove what has already washed up, and when volunteers record what they pick up, the counts help cities and researchers see where it comes from.

Surfrider Foundation
Ocean Conservancy

Dunes

Dune plants hold sand in place and give shelter to wildlife. Invasive plants, such as European beachgrass, can change how a dune is shaped, which is why dune work often means pulling invaders and planting natives.

California Invasive Plant Council

Creeks and streams

Creeks carry runoff to the bay and the ocean. Streamside plants shade the water, slow erosion and give birds and insects a home, so planting and trash removal along a creek helps everything downstream.

San Francisco Estuary Partnership

Wetlands and the bay

Much of San Francisco Bay's tidal marsh was filled or diked off over the past two centuries. Restoring it brings back habitat for birds and fish, and healthy marshes can also help soften the effects of rising water along the shoreline.

Save The Bay
San Francisco Estuary Partnership

Woods and hills

Invasive plants such as French broom and English ivy can crowd out native plants across open space and preserves. Regular pulls, followed by replanting, give native species room to return.

California Invasive Plant Council
California Native Plant Society

Gardens and nurseries

Native plants grown in nurseries end up in restoration sites, and native gardens feed pollinators such as bees and butterflies. It is gentle, family-friendly work with a clear result.

California Native Plant Society
Xerces Society
